Law School GPA Requirements: What You Actually Need to Get In (2026 Guide)

Last updated: August 19, 2026

T14 medians, CAS GPA explained, school-by-school data, LSAT interaction, and strategies for low GPA applicants.

By Anwaar Ahmad | Last Updated: August 2026 | Data Source: ABA 509 Disclosures 2025–2026

Quick Answer

Law school GPA requirements depend on the school tier. Top 14 law schools have median GPAs of 3.84 to 3.97. Most law schools require a minimum of 3.0. CAS GPA — calculated by LSAC from all undergraduate transcripts — is what law schools actually use, not your transcript GPA.

What GPA Do You Need for Law School?

If you're a pre-law student staring at your transcript and wondering whether your GPA is good enough, you're not alone. GPA is one of the two most important numbers in any law school application — the other being your LSAT score — and understanding exactly where you stand can make or break your school list.

So, what GPA do you need for law school? The honest answer: it depends heavily on where you're applying. A 3.5 GPA can make you a competitive applicant at a solid regional law school but leave you below the 25th percentile at a T14 program. A 3.9 might be strong at most schools but still feel tight at Yale or Harvard.

This guide breaks down law school GPA requirements by school tier, covers specific numbers for the top programs, explains how GPA and LSAT interact, and tells you exactly what to do if your GPA isn't where you want it to be.

Quick answer: Most ABA-accredited law schools accept students with GPAs between 2.7 and 4.0. The median GPA at T14 schools ranges from 3.71 to 3.96. At regional and lower-ranked schools, medians typically fall between 3.0 and 3.5.

What Is CAS GPA and How Is It Different From Your Transcript GPA?

Before diving into numbers, you need to understand one thing that surprises almost every pre-law student: the GPA law schools see is not the GPA on your college transcript.

LSAC (Law School Admission Council) recalculates your GPA using their own system, called the Credential Assembly Service (CAS) GPA.

Here's how CAS GPA works differently from your transcript GPA:

How LSAC Calculates Your CAS GPA

  • Every undergraduate grade counts. LSAC pulls grades from every undergraduate institution you attended — including community colleges, summer programs, study abroad courses, and any school you transferred from. Your home institution may have forgiven a repeated course; LSAC will not.
  • Grade replacement policies don't apply. If your school lets you retake a D in Chemistry and replace it with an A, your transcript shows only the A. LSAC counts both the D and the A in your CAS GPA.
  • Withdrawals and incompletes are reviewed. A pattern of late withdrawals can flag your application even if they don't appear in your official GPA.
  • International grades are converted. If you studied abroad and received foreign grades, LSAC converts them to its own scale.
  • The 4.0 scale is standardized. LSAC converts all grades to a consistent 4.0 scale, regardless of your school's grading system.

Why This Matters

Your CAS GPA can be notably different from your transcript GPA — sometimes lower (if you've retaken courses or transferred with poor grades), occasionally higher (if your school uses a deflated grading scale).

Always calculate your CAS GPA before building your school list. Notes on Reading These Numbers

Yale law school GPA and Harvard law school GPA are nearly identical at the top — both hover around 3.91–3.92 at the median. The difference between these schools in practice comes down to LSAT, letters of recommendation, personal statements, and fit.

Stanford law school GPA requirements are similarly elite, with a 3.90 median. Stanford is known for a slightly more holistic review than some peers, which means exceptional LSAT scores or unique backgrounds can sometimes offset a GPA that sits at the lower end of their range.

Georgetown has the most accessible GPA profile among the T14, which partly reflects its larger class size — Georgetown enrolls more students than most T14 schools, giving it more room to take calculated risks on applicants with mixed profiles.

Law School GPA and LSAT: How They Work Together

Here's something many pre-law students get wrong: admissions offices don't look at GPA and LSAT in isolation. They look at both together, and the combination tells a very different story depending on the school.

The Basic Interaction

Most law schools use an index score — a weighted formula that combines GPA and LSAT into a single number for initial screening. The exact weights vary by school, but LSAT typically carries slightly more weight than GPA because it's standardized across all applicants.

How Schools Weight GPA vs. LSAT

Schools are also aware of USNWR rankings, which use median GPA and LSAT of enrolled students. This creates an incentive for schools to protect their medians — which means a very high LSAT can sometimes offset a lower GPA more effectively than the reverse, because LSAT has historically carried slightly more ranking weight.

The Splitter Strategy: Low GPA, High LSAT

A splitter in law school admissions is someone whose LSAT score is significantly stronger than their GPA would suggest — usually defined as having an LSAT at or above a school's median while the GPA falls below the 25th percentile.

Why the Splitter Strategy Works (Sometimes)

Because LSAT scores have historically carried heavy weight in law school rankings metrics, schools sometimes admit splitters strategically to protect their LSAT median. A student with a 177 LSAT and a 3.4 GPA might be admitted to a school where the median GPA is 3.85 — not in spite of the low GPA, but because the school calculates that the LSAT contribution is worth the GPA drag.

Splitter Success Factors

Not all splitters are equal. Here's what determines whether the strategy works:

LSAT Score Required

To be a meaningful splitter at T14 schools, you generally need an LSAT score at or above the 90th percentile (approximately 170+). A "high" LSAT of 165 won't move the needle at Yale, but it might work very well at a T25 school where the median LSAT is 163.

GPA Floor

Most T14 schools have an informal floor around 3.0–3.3 for splitters, regardless of LSAT. Below that, even a perfect 180 won't carry the application at elite programs — though some T25–T50 schools remain viable.

School Selection

Some schools are more splitter-friendly than others. Georgetown, Cornell, and UCLA have historically shown willingness to admit splitters with strong LSATs. Yale and Stanford are less predictable — they review files holistically and may weight GPA more heavily for "reverse splitters" (high GPA, lower LSAT).

The Narrative

A low GPA almost always requires explanation. Illness, family hardship, a difficult major, or a clear upward trend can help contextualize numbers that look weak on paper. An addendum that explains (without making excuses) what happened and what you learned is almost always worth writing.

Is the Splitter Strategy Right for You?

If you've already graduated and your GPA is fixed, focusing on maximizing your LSAT is the highest-leverage thing you can do. Use a GPA Simulator to understand your exact GPA situation, then channel your energy into test prep.

If you're still in school, however, see the next section — there may be more you can do on the GPA side than you think.

Can You Get Into Law School With a Low GPA?

Yes — but "low" is relative, and strategy matters.

Define "Low" for Your Target Schools

A 3.2 GPA is below the 25th percentile at Georgetown but above the median at many solid regional law schools. The first step is matching realistic expectations to your actual number. Pull up the ABA 509 data for schools you're considering and find where your GPA falls in each enrolled class.

Pathways for Low-GPA Applicants

1. Ace the LSAT

The single most effective way to compensate for a low GPA is a high LSAT score. This is the splitter path described above. LSAT prep is time-intensive but highly coachable — unlike GPA, which is largely fixed once you've graduated.

2. Show an Upward Trend

If your GPA dipped freshman year and recovered strongly, many schools will note the trend. Some addmissions officers specifically look for semesters where performance improved. A rising trajectory signals maturity and resilience.

3. Write a Strong Addendum

A GPA addendum is a brief (usually one paragraph to one page) explanation of why your GPA doesn't reflect your abilities. Valid reasons include documented illness, family emergencies, mental health challenges, financial stress that required full-time work, or a late transition from a demanding major. Keep it factual, honest, and forward-looking.

4. Target Schools Where Your GPA Is Competitive

Build a realistic school list with a true safety school — one where your GPA and LSAT are above the 75th percentile. Being realistic about your chances isn't pessimistic; it's strategic.

5. Consider a Post-Bacc or Master's Program

Some applicants with genuinely low undergraduate GPAs pursue a post-baccalaureate or master's program to demonstrate graduate-level academic ability. LSAC does not include graduate grades in your CAS GPA, but a strong graduate transcript can be referenced in your personal statement and may be reviewed favorably by admissions officers.

6. Gain Meaningful Work Experience

A few years of substantive work experience — especially in law, policy, advocacy, or a field directly relevant to your intended legal career — can meaningfully strengthen a low-GPA application. It reframes the narrative: you're not a struggling student anymore, you're a professional with a clear direction.

How to Improve Your GPA Before Applying

If you're still enrolled and have semesters left before applying, you have real options. Here's how to make the most of the time you have.

Calculate Where You Stand Right Now

Before doing anything, get an accurate picture of your current GPA and what's possible. Use the GPA Calculator to find your current cumulative GPA, and use the GPA Simulator to model different scenarios — for example, "What happens to my GPA if I get straight A's for two more semesters?"

The simulator is especially useful for understanding diminishing returns: the more credits you've already earned, the harder it becomes to move the needle. 40 credits of A's can transform a 2.8 GPA early in college; the same effort applied in your senior year after 100 credits may only move a 3.3 to a 3.45.

Practical Steps to Raise Your GPA

Take a realistic course load. Overloading on credits while working part-time and doing extracurriculars is a recipe for mediocre grades across the board. For your final pre-application semesters, prioritize depth over breadth.

Retake failed or near-failed courses strategically. Remember that LSAC counts both grades in your CAS GPA — but if a retake genuinely demonstrates mastery, many admissions officers will respect the effort even if the math doesn't fully recover.

Go to office hours. This is unglamorous advice, but consistently showing up to office hours and engaging with professors tends to produce better grades, better letters of recommendation, and a clearer sense of the material.

Choose your electives carefully. If you're genuinely interested in a topic, you'll likely perform better in it. Don't take a "bird course" you hate in hopes of an easy A — your engagement will suffer and so will your grade.

Build in buffer time before applying. Many students apply during their junior or early senior year when they still have semesters left. If your GPA is borderline, it may be worth delaying application by a year to complete additional strong semesters — especially if the LSAT prep time is valuable too.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the minimum GPA for law school?

There's no universal minimum GPA required to apply to law school. ABA-accredited law schools accept students across a wide range, and some regional programs regularly admit students with GPAs below 3.0. That said, below a 2.5, your options become significantly limited. T14 programs rarely admit anyone below 3.5, regardless of other factors.

2. Is a 3.5 GPA good for law school?

A 3.5 GPA is competitive at many law schools, including strong regional and T50 programs. At T14 schools, a 3.5 places you below the 25th percentile — meaning you'd need a very high LSAT (170+) to have a realistic shot and would be applying as a splitter. At T25–T50 programs, a 3.5 is solid.

3. Is a 3.7 GPA good for law school?

A 3.7 GPA puts you in competitive range at many T14 schools, particularly if your LSAT is strong. It's above the 25th percentile at most T14 programs and at or near the median at some (like Georgetown and UVA). Paired with a 170+ LSAT, a 3.7 is a genuinely competitive application at the full T14.

4. What is a good GPA for Harvard Law School?

The median GPA for enrolled Harvard Law students in 2025–2026 is approximately 3.91, with a 25th percentile of 3.81. Harvard also places enormous weight on LSAT scores (median around 174). A 3.7 GPA is possible at Harvard if your LSAT is exceptional (175+), but the odds are challenging.

5. Does law school GPA matter after you graduate?

Your undergraduate GPA matters for law school admissions — full stop. Once you're in law school, your law school GPA matters for things like Law Review, judicial clerkships, and big law recruiting. Your undergraduate GPA fades in relevance quickly after you're admitted.

6. What is the average GPA for T14 law schools?

Based on 2025–2026 ABA 509 data, the median GPA across T14 law schools is approximately 3.80, ranging from around 3.76 at Georgetown to 3.92 at Yale and Harvard. The 25th percentile across the T14 generally falls around 3.65.

7. Can I get into law school with a 2.5 GPA?

It's difficult but not impossible. With a 2.5 GPA, you'll be below the 25th percentile at almost every ABA-accredited program. A very high LSAT score (165+), significant upward grade trend, or compelling life circumstances can help at lower-ranked regional schools. T100 admissions become very difficult, and T50 is essentially out of reach.

8. Does LSAC include graduate school grades in my CAS GPA?

No. LSAC only calculates CAS GPA from undergraduate coursework. Graduate school grades (master's degrees, professional degrees, etc.) are not included in the formula. However, graduate transcripts are still submitted through LSAC and reviewed by admissions offices — they just don't change the official CAS GPA number.

9. How does LSAC calculate GPA for pass/fail courses?

Pass/fail courses do not factor into your CAS GPA calculation at all. Only letter-graded courses are included. This means courses taken pass/fail during COVID-19 policy periods, study abroad, or elective pass/fail selections won't positively or negatively affect your CAS GPA.

10. What is a splitter in law school admissions?

A splitter is an applicant whose LSAT score is significantly higher than their GPA would predict. For example, someone with a 3.3 GPA and a 175 LSAT is a "high splitter." The inverse — high GPA, lower LSAT — is sometimes called a "reverse splitter." Splitter applicants have inconsistent results because different schools weight these metrics differently, but a high enough LSAT can open doors at schools where the GPA would typically disqualify an applicant.

11. Is a 3.9 GPA good enough for Yale Law School?

A 3.9 GPA is excellent and puts you above Yale's median (3.92) only by a small margin — so you'd be in solid shape on GPA. But Yale's process is notably holistic and extremely selective: they receive thousands of qualified applicants each cycle. A 3.9 + 175 LSAT is a competitive application, but nothing guarantees admission.

12. Should I use a GPA calculator or GPA simulator for law school planning?

Both serve different purposes. A GPA calculator (GPA Calculator) tells you your current GPA based on the grades you've already earned. A GPA simulator (GPA Simulator) helps you project what your GPA will be after future semesters based on assumed grades — which is the more useful tool for planning purposes. If you're still in school, the simulator is invaluable for understanding what's realistically achievable before your application is due.
