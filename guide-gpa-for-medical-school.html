Medical School GPA Requirements: Everything Pre-Med Students Need to Know in 2026

Last updated: August 19, 2026

Average MD and DO GPA benchmarks, science GPA (BCPM) explained, school-by-school expectations, and practical ways to strengthen a low GPA.

By Anwaar Ahmad | Last Updated: August 2026 | Data Source: AAMC MSAR 2025–2026

What GPA Do You Need for Medical School?

If you've been asking yourself "what GPA do you need for med school in 2026?" — you're not alone. It's one of the most searched questions among pre-med students, and the honest answer is: it depends on where you want to go.

The short version: for most MD programs, you want a cumulative GPA of at least 3.5, and ideally 3.7 or above. For top-tier schools like Harvard, Stanford, or NYU, you're competing against students with GPAs in the 3.9 range. For DO programs, the bar is a bit more accessible, with most successful matriculants landing between 3.5 and 3.65.

But here's what most articles don't tell you: the number itself is only half the picture. Medical schools look at what kind of GPA you have, how your grades trended over time, and how your GPA pairs with your MCAT score. A student with a 3.6 GPA and a 515 MCAT is often more competitive than one with a 3.9 GPA and a 503.

This guide breaks down the medical school GPA requirements you need to know — from national averages to school-specific data — so you can set realistic goals and build a smart application strategy.

Average GPA for Medical School Applicants vs. Matriculants

One of the most important distinctions to understand early on is the difference between applicants and matriculants.

  • Applicants are everyone who submits an application to MD programs.
  • Matriculants are the students who actually got in and enrolled.

These two groups have very different GPA profiles, and understanding that gap is key to figuring out where you stand.

According to AAMC FACTS data for the 2025–2026 application cycle:

The average GPA for medical school applicants in the 2025–2026 cycle was 3.67, while matriculants averaged 3.81 — a gap of 0.14 points that reflects how significantly GPA filters the applicant pool. On the MCAT side, the mean score for matriculants climbed to 512.1, while all applicants (including those not accepted) averaged 506.3.

What Is Science GPA (BCPM) and Why Does It Matter?

When you apply to medical school through AMCAS (the American Medical College Application Service), your GPA is actually calculated in two separate ways:

  1. Cumulative GPA — every undergraduate course you've taken
  2. Science GPA (BCPM) — only courses in Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Mathematics

BCPM stands for Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Mathematics, and it's often called the "science GPA" in pre-med circles.

Why Do Schools Weigh It Separately?

Medical schools care deeply about your BCPM GPA because it directly reflects your ability to handle the rigorous science curriculum of med school. Organic chemistry, biochemistry, physiology — these are the building blocks of clinical medicine, and your BCPM GPA tells admissions committees whether you've proven you can handle them.

AMCAS automatically calculates your BCPM GPA from your transcripts. A student with a 3.9 cumulative GPA but a 3.4 BCPM GPA (because they took many non-science courses to boost their overall average) will be scrutinized in admissions review.

Cumulative GPA vs. Science GPA: Understanding the Difference

Here's what to know:

  • Your cumulative GPA includes everything — science, humanities, electives, you name it.
  • Your BCPM GPA is a subset, covering only the core science and math courses.
  • Most students have a slightly lower science GPA than cumulative GPA.
  • Nationally, the gap between matriculant overall GPA (3.81) and science GPA (3.75) is roughly 0.06 points.

Key Takeaways by Tier

Top 20 Programs: A GPA below 3.8 is below the average for most of these schools. That doesn't mean you can't apply, but you'll need exceptional compensating factors — outstanding MCAT, significant research, or a uniquely compelling personal story.

State Schools: Many state programs heavily favor in-state applicants and may weigh community ties, rural background, or mission fit heavily. A 3.6 GPA from an in-state applicant at a state school is often more competitive than the national average suggests.

DO Programs: The average DO matriculant GPA has been rising. DO programs typically admit students with GPAs closer to 3.5–3.65, making them an important option if your GPA or MCAT falls below the MD threshold.

School-Specific Highlights

Harvard Medical School GPA

The median GPA of students admitted to Harvard Medical School is 3.96 and the median MCAT score is 521, with section scores averaging between 129 and 131. Harvard Medical School does not publish a minimum GPA requirement — overall GPA is one factor in a holistic review that also considers MCAT scores, research, clinical experience, service, leadership, and personal qualities. In practical terms, most admitted students have GPAs at or above 3.9.

Stanford Medical School GPA Requirements

The average GPA of enrolled students at Stanford School of Medicine is 3.95 and the average MCAT score is 520. Stanford does not publish a minimum GPA requirement, but the data makes clear that near-perfect academics are expected. Applicants below a 3.85 overall GPA face a significant challenge unless other elements of the application are truly exceptional.

UCLA Medical School GPA

UCLA David Geffen School of Medicine is one of the most competitive public medical schools in the country. UCLA DGSOM consistently receives 11,000–14,000+ applications each year for roughly 175 seats. Over the past six admissions cycles, the acceptance rate has hovered between 1.22% and 1.56%, making it one of the most selective medical schools in the country. The average GPA for matriculants falls in the 3.85–3.96 range depending on the source, with an MCAT average around 515–522.

NYU Grossman Medical School GPA Requirements

NYU Grossman is notable not only for its stellar admissions profile, but for offering full-tuition scholarships to all MD students. The median and average undergraduate GPAs for students in the most recent incoming class at NYU Grossman were 3.98 and 3.96, respectively. The average MCAT for matriculants at NYU Grossman is 523, making it one of the highest-scoring incoming classes in the country.

Johns Hopkins School of Medicine GPA

The average GPA of enrolled students at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine is 3.94 and the average MCAT score is 520. Hopkins emphasizes a rigorous research and clinical foundation and is particularly competitive for students interested in physician-scientist tracks.

Duke University School of Medicine GPA

The average GPA of enrolled students at Duke University School of Medicine is 3.92 and the average MCAT score is 520, with an acceptance rate of 1.33%. Duke's unique curriculum compresses preclinical training into the first year, giving students an entire third year for research or dual-degree work — making research experience especially valued.

Medical School GPA and MCAT: How They Work Together

Your GPA and MCAT score don't exist in isolation — they interact. Understanding this relationship can help you make smarter decisions about where to focus your energy.

The Interplay Between GPA and MCAT

Think of your GPA and MCAT as two pillars of the same application. When one is stronger, it can partially offset weakness in the other — but there are limits.

No matter how many medical schools point to their holistic admissions process, the trend of ever-increasing stats among matriculating students shows no signs of slowing down. Written materials such as personal statements and letters of recommendation cannot overcome low stats.

Here's a rough guide to how GPA and MCAT interact:

GPA Trend Matters

Admissions committees don't just look at your final number — they look at how your grades developed over time. An upward trend (e.g., a 3.2 freshman year improving to a 3.8 junior and senior year) is viewed much more favorably than a flat or declining trajectory. If you struggled early but found your footing, make sure your application addresses that arc clearly.

When to Retake Classes vs. Focus on MCAT

If your GPA is below 3.5, strengthening it is important — but your options may be limited depending on how close you are to graduation. Your MCAT, on the other hand, is retakable and can be improved with focused preparation. A dramatically higher MCAT can sometimes compensate for a lower GPA, especially at mid-tier programs.

Can You Get Into Medical School With a Low GPA?

Yes — but the path is narrower, and it requires a deliberate strategy.

First, let's define "low." In the context of medical school admissions, a low GPA is generally considered anything below 3.5. Below 3.0 is very difficult for any LCME-accredited MD program. That said, people with sub-3.5 GPAs do get into medical school every year, particularly when other parts of their application are strong.

What "Low GPA" Actually Means for Medical School

As GPAs continue to rise (likely due to grade inflation), the typical GPA advisors recommend is a minimum of 3.6 across all subjects. However, many students with lower GPAs are accepted.

Here are the realistic scenarios for low GPA applicants:

GPA 3.3–3.5: You're below average for most MD programs. A very high MCAT (514+) and compelling application narrative can get you into some MD programs. DO programs become your primary realistic target alongside those MD programs.

GPA 3.0–3.3: MD acceptance is rare and typically requires exceptional MCAT performance (515+), significant upward trend, and very strong extracurriculars. DO programs and post-baccalaureate pathways become the primary route.

GPA Below 3.0: Most accredited MD programs screen applicants out at this threshold automatically. Even many DO programs set a 3.0 floor. A formal post-baccalaureate or Special Master's Program (SMP) is typically necessary before reapplying.

Strategies That Work for Low GPA Applicants

Post-Baccalaureate Programs: "Post-bacc" programs let you retake or supplement science courses as a non-degree student. Strong performance in a post-bacc shows adcoms you've addressed earlier academic struggles.

Special Master's Programs (SMPs): These graduate-level science programs are designed specifically for pre-med students who need to demonstrate academic readiness. They're often "linkage" programs with medical schools, and a strong GPA in an SMP carries significant weight.

Upward GPA Trend: If your grades were poor early but steadily improved, be explicit about this in your personal statement and secondary essays. Committees look at trends, not just final averages.

Exceptional MCAT Performance: A high MCAT (514+) is one of the strongest ways to compensate for a lower GPA. It directly demonstrates that you can master the scientific knowledge medical school demands.

Mission Fit: Schools with specific missions — primary care, rural medicine, underserved communities — may deprioritize raw stats in favor of applicants whose background and goals align with the school's mission.

DO Programs: Being open to DO programs significantly expands your options. DO acceptance rates tend to be higher, and DO programs have lower median MCAT and GPA requirements, tend to conduct more holistic reviews, and have more programs accepting more students per cycle. Both MD and DO graduates hold full medical licenses and practice in all specialties.

How to Improve Your GPA Before Applying

If you're not happy with where your GPA stands right now, you have options. The most important thing is to act early — GPA improvement takes time, and the best time to start is well before your application cycle.

1. Identify Your Problem Areas

Before anything else, pull up your transcript and look at where your grades are dragging you down. Are they concentrated in one or two difficult science classes? Are they scattered across multiple semesters? Knowing the pattern helps you choose the right fix.

2. Retake Key Science Courses

Most medical schools (and AMCAS) include all grades in your GPA calculation, even retakes. However, a higher grade in a retaken course shows improvement and replaces the narrative of the original poor grade. Focus on foundational science prerequisites: general chemistry, organic chemistry, biology, and physics.

3. Take Additional Upper-Level Science Courses

Rather than retaking old courses, some students supplement their transcript with upper-level science electives — biochemistry, cell biology, genetics, physiology. Doing well in these demonstrates that you've grown academically.

4. Consider a Post-Baccalaureate Program

A structured post-bacc program is particularly helpful if you have multiple weak science grades. These programs are designed around pre-med needs and are recognized by medical school admissions offices.

5. Enroll in a Special Master's Program (SMP)

If you're a recent graduate with a low overall GPA, an SMP is often the most powerful signal you can send. Graduate-level science performance is weighted heavily because it directly predicts medical school performance.

6. Use a GPA Simulator Before You Apply

Before committing to a strategy, model the outcomes. How many A's would it take to raise your GPA from 3.3 to 3.5? What happens if you complete 30 additional credit hours with a 3.9?

7. Don't Wait — Apply Strategically

If you've done what you can to improve your GPA and you're approaching a reasonable target, don't wait forever for a "perfect" number. Apply to a well-researched list that includes programs where your current stats are at or above the 25th percentile. Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the minimum GPA for medical school?

Most LCME-accredited MD programs don't publish an official minimum GPA, but many use 3.0 as an internal screening cutoff. In practice, applicants with a total GPA below 3.60 face a more challenging path to medical school and should focus their school list on programs that weigh MCAT performance highly or specifically serve under-matched applicants. For DO programs, 3.0 is a common stated minimum, with competitive applicants typically at 3.5 or above.

2. What is the average GPA for medical school in 2025–2026?

The mean overall GPA for 2025 matriculants rose to 3.81, up from 3.79 in 2024. The median overall GPA for matriculants is now 3.87. For all applicants (including those who weren't accepted), the average was 3.67.

3. What is a good science GPA for medical school?

The average matriculant science GPA (BCPM) at U.S. allopathic medical schools was 3.75 for the 2025–2026 application cycle. A BCPM GPA of 3.65 or above is generally considered competitive for MD programs. Below 3.5, a strong MCAT becomes increasingly important to compensate.

4. Does my MCAT score matter more than my GPA?

They matter roughly equally, but neither can fully substitute for the other. Both your GPA and MCAT score are important factors in medical school admissions decisions, and most medical schools use a holistic review process that takes into account many factors beyond GPA and MCAT scores. That said, the MCAT is often considered more "correctable" since it can be retaken, while GPA is harder to change quickly.

5. What is the BCPM GPA, and which courses count?

BCPM stands for Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Mathematics. AMCAS includes all college-level courses in these disciplines when calculating your science GPA. This includes labs, upper-division science courses, and any science courses taken at community college. Courses in psychology, sociology, or health sciences may or may not count, depending on how they're classified in your transcript.

6. Is a 3.5 GPA good enough for medical school?

A 3.5 GPA is below the national average for MD matriculants (3.81) and puts you in a competitive position primarily for mid-tier to lower-ranked MD programs and DO programs. With a strong MCAT (510+), meaningful clinical experience, and a well-crafted application, a 3.5 is not a disqualifier — but your school list needs to be realistic. Use the GPA Calculator to understand exactly where your numbers stand relative to each school you're considering.

7. What GPA do I need for DO medical school?

According to AACOM data from the 2025 application cycle, the average GPA for matriculants to DO schools was 3.63, and the average MCAT for DO matriculants was approximately 503. Competitive applicants typically have a GPA of 3.5 or above and an MCAT of 500 or higher.

8. What is the average GPA at Harvard Medical School?

The average GPA for enrolled students at Harvard Medical School is 3.9 and the average MCAT score is 520. Some sources report the median accepted GPA as high as 3.96. Harvard does not publish a minimum GPA requirement.

9. What is Stanford Medical School's GPA requirement?

The average GPA of enrolled students at Stanford School of Medicine is 3.95. Stanford does not publish a minimum GPA requirement, but near-perfect academic performance is effectively expected for competitive candidates.

10. Can I get into medical school with a 3.0 GPA?

It is very difficult, but not impossible — particularly for DO programs or schools with specific mission focus areas. A 3.0 GPA with a very high MCAT and compelling extracurricular story may get you into a small number of programs. However, most advisors recommend a post-baccalaureate or SMP program first to demonstrate you can handle graduate-level coursework. Use the GPA Simulator to map out what it would take to raise your GPA to a more competitive range before applying.

11. Does where I went to undergrad affect how my GPA is viewed?

Yes and no. Grade inflation is real, and admissions committees are aware that a 3.9 from one school isn't identical to a 3.9 from another. Your course rigor and major matter too — a 3.7 in biochemistry from a research university often reads differently than a 3.9 from a less rigorous program. The MCAT helps standardize this, which is one reason medical schools weigh it so heavily.

12. What if my GPA is low in one semester but high overall?

Admissions committees look at semester-by-semester performance, not just your final number. A single difficult semester due to a personal hardship, illness, or poor course selection early in college is not necessarily damaging — especially if you rebounded strongly. Address any significant dips directly in your application's additional comments section or personal statement. An upward trend is one of the most compelling academic narratives you can present.
