Cum Laude, Magna Cum Laude & Summa Cum Laude GPA Requirements (2026)

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Typical GPA ranges for each Latin honor, university-specific thresholds from official registrar pages, how class-rank systems work, and how to list honors on your resume.

By Anwaar Ahmad | Updated 2026 | Data Source: Official University Registrar Pages, 2025–2026

Graduating with Latin honors is one of the most visible marks of academic achievement a college student can earn. You've probably heard the terms — cum laude, magna cum laude, summa cum laude — announced at graduation ceremonies or printed on resumes. But what do they actually mean, and what GPA do you need to earn them?

The answers are more nuanced than most students expect. There's no universal standard. Your school sets its own rules, and the GPA required for each honor can vary significantly depending on whether your school uses a fixed threshold or a class-rank system.

This guide breaks it all down — including university-specific data sourced from official registrar pages, current as of 2025–2026 — so you know exactly what you're aiming for. Always verify with your own registrar, as thresholds are recalculated annually.

Quick Answer

Cum laude typically requires a GPA of 3.5 to 3.64. Magna cum laude requires 3.65 to 3.79. Summa cum laude requires 3.8 to 4.0. Exact thresholds vary by university — some schools use class rank instead of fixed GPA cutoffs.

What Are Latin Honors?

Latin honors are academic distinctions awarded at graduation to undergraduate students who have achieved a high cumulative GPA. The terms come from Latin: cum laude means "with honor," magna cum laude means "with great honor," and summa cum laude means "with highest honor." They have been part of US commencement ceremonies since the late nineteenth century, borrowed from the European university tradition.

There are three tiers, stacked from lowest to highest:

Cum Laude ("With Honor")

This is the entry-level Latin honor. It recognizes students who performed well above the average student body — typically finishing in the top 20–30% of their graduating class. To qualify for cum laude, a student must typically have a GPA of 3.50–3.69 out of the 4.00 scale. Think of it as the foundation — impressive, meaningful, and attainable for students who've consistently put in strong work across all four years.

Magna Cum Laude ("With Great Honor")

The middle tier, and a meaningful distinction that can strengthen job applications and graduate school profiles. Magna cum laude means "with great honor" and usually needs a GPA of 3.7 to 3.9, recognizing consistently excellent grades. Its requirements vary by school, but it's typically designated for students in the top 6–15% of a class or those with a GPA of 3.7–3.8.

Summa Cum Laude ("With Highest Honor")

The pinnacle of undergraduate academic achievement. Summa cum laude typically requires a cumulative GPA of 3.9 or above on the 4.0 scale. Some institutions require 3.95 or higher. Others like Harvard and Yale use class rank and award summa to the top 5% of graduates regardless of absolute GPA.

These honors appear on your official diploma, your final transcript, and are typically read aloud at graduation. They're permanent — there's no way to lose them after you graduate.

One important note: not every university uses Latin honors. MIT uses alternative recognition, Stanford uses "With Distinction," UC schools use "Highest Distinction," and UT Austin uses "Highest Honors." If your school uses a different system, this guide still gives you the context to understand how your designation compares.

Why GPA Thresholds Vary by School

If you've been trying to google "what GPA is cum laude," you've probably found conflicting answers. That's because there's no national standard, and schools use two fundamentally different methods to determine who earns Latin honors.

Method 1: Fixed GPA Thresholds

The most common method. The university sets a fixed GPA minimum — for example, 3.5 for cum laude. Any student meeting that threshold at graduation receives the honor. Advantages: predictable — students can plan toward specific GPA targets throughout their degree. Objective, with no ambiguity.

This is what most students imagine when they think of Latin honors. Set a GPA target, hit it by graduation, earn the honor. Schools like UConn, the University of Michigan (Engineering), and many mid-sized public universities use this system, sometimes with a minimum class-rank requirement layered on top.

The downside: grade inflation can quietly make this system less meaningful over time. If average GPAs at a school creep up from 3.0 to 3.4 over decades, far more students will clear a fixed 3.5 threshold — which is exactly what happened at Harvard in the 1990s.

Method 2: Class-Rank Percentile (Slice System)

At some universities, particularly those with large graduating classes, Latin honors are awarded to fixed percentages of graduates rather than based on GPA cutoffs. Harvard uses class rank percentile. The top 4–5% of each graduating class earns summa cum laude, the top 15% earns magna cum laude, and the top 30% earns cum laude.

Under this system, the actual GPA required moves every year depending on how your entire graduating class performed. In a particularly strong class, you might need a 3.99 to place in the top 5%. In a more average year, a 3.96 might do it. You won't know the exact cutoff until after you graduate.

Class-rank institutions maintain consistent rarity by design — exactly 5% receive summa every year regardless of cohort ability. A summa from a class-rank school therefore carries a precise rarity signal that GPA threshold schools cannot replicate.

How the Two Systems Affect You

If your school uses fixed GPA thresholds, you have a clear target from day one. Use Scholarics' check your GPA instantly to find out your current cumulative GPA and how close you are to the threshold.

If your school uses the percentile system, you're essentially competing against your classmates. That makes it harder to plan precisely, but it also means the honor will remain genuinely rare no matter how high grades trend across campus.

Many schools blend both: they require both a minimum GPA floor and a minimum class rank. UConn, for example, does exactly this (see the university table below).

A few things to note from this table:

Harvard's system is famously complex. To be eligible for a degree summa cum laude, you must receive "Highest Honors" within your department, and your cumulative GPA must be above the university's cutoff for a summa degree. The department sets the limit on the highest level of Latin honors you can receive. In May 2025, Harvard's class-rank system worked out to 3.989 for summa, 3.931 for magna, and 3.762 for cum laude.

MIT and Stanford don't use Latin honors at all. Stanford does not use Latin honors (summa cum laude, magna cum laude, cum laude), but does award "Distinction" based on GPA to graduating seniors. If you go to MIT or Stanford, your school uses an equivalent system under a different name — make sure you know what that is.

UCLA's thresholds are high and school-specific. Students eligible for 2025–26 honors at UCLA must have completed 90 or more units for a letter grade at the University of California and must have attained a cumulative GPA that places them in the top 20 percent of the school (GPA of 3.887 or better) for cum laude, the top 10 percent (GPA of 3.939 or better) for magna cum laude, and the top five percent (GPA of 3.974 or better) for summa cum laude.

University of Michigan varies wildly by school. The College of Engineering uses fixed thresholds: 3.20–3.49 for cum laude, 3.50–3.74 for magna cum laude, and 3.75–4.00 for summa cum laude. However, the Ross School of Business uses a percentile system. If you're at Michigan, your school and program matter enormously.

UMD uses a hybrid approach. Summa cum laude is awarded to students with a GPA equal to or greater than the highest two percent of GPAs from the specific college or school averaged over the previous academic year; magna cum laude to the next highest three percent; and cum laude to the following five percent. They also must receive at least a 3.9 GPA.

Georgetown uses pure percentile rankings. Georgetown calculates Latin honors within designated percentiles of the current cumulative GPA of the senior class in each school: the lowest GPA of the top five percent to graduate summa cum laude, the next ten percent for magna cum laude, and the next ten percent for cum laude.

A few clarifications worth noting:

The jump from cum laude to magna cum laude is significant. Magna cum laude signals top 10–20% academic achievement — a strong credential for virtually any opportunity. For competitive employers in finance, consulting, and law, magna is the threshold where honors start to feel like a real differentiator on a resume.

The jump from magna to summa is even harder. Approximately 3–8% of US college graduates receive summa cum laude designation. At a graduating class of 1,000, that is roughly 30–80 students. It represents near-perfect academic performance sustained across four years — not just one or two great semesters.

Some schools require extra work for the highest honors. Many universities require an honors thesis, senior capstone, or approved research project for magna or summa, on top of the GPA requirement. Always check your school's specific bulletin.

Do Latin Honors Matter for Jobs and Grad School?

This is probably the question you actually care about, and the honest answer is: it depends on the field and how long ago you graduated.

For Competitive Entry-Level Jobs

Latin honors matter most for competitive entry-level positions. Investment banking, consulting, law, and finance often screen for GPA minimums — honors signal that threshold has been met. Outside these sectors, honors are a positive indicator but rarely decisive.

Some competitive finance and consulting recruiting processes use GPA thresholds, particularly for entry-level candidates. Latin honors (especially magna or summa) can function as a shorthand signal that you've cleared a strong academic bar without the recruiter having to parse every transcript line.

In tech, creative fields, nursing, education, and most other industries, honors matter much less. Skills, portfolio, and internship experience tend to dominate.

For Graduate School

For law school, the picture is clear: T14 programs (Harvard, Yale, Columbia, etc.) have median admitted GPAs of 3.7–3.9. Summa cum laude signals readiness for law school rigor and is a strong differentiator.

For medical school, medical schools evaluate overall GPA and science GPA (BCPM). A 3.9+ GPA with summa cum laude from a rigorous program is a major strength in medical school applications, particularly combined with strong MCAT scores.

For PhD programs, the prestige of your institution and the quality of your research and recommendation letters tend to matter more than the specific honor tier. For Ivy League PhDs, research publications and recommendations often matter more than magna versus summa distinction.

How Long Should You Keep It on Your Resume?

There's no hard rule here, but the general pattern is this: Latin honors carry the most weight early in your career, when you don't yet have a long professional track record to point to. As your work experience grows, it gradually becomes less central to how you present yourself. In academic and research careers it is appropriate to include it indefinitely. In most professional careers it becomes less relevant as work experience accumulates.

One more thing worth knowing: a Harvard magna cum laude (top 20% of Harvard graduates) is extremely competitive. Hiring managers at elite firms who attended selective universities understand this context. The institution's reputation amplifies the meaning of each honor level. A summa from a flagship state university carries a different signal than a cum laude from a highly selective private school — and experienced recruiters know the difference.

How to Check If You're On Track

If you're currently in college and wondering whether Latin honors are within reach, here's a practical approach:

Step 1: Find Your School's Exact Requirements

Go to your registrar's website and search for "Latin honors" or "graduation honors." Most schools publish either a fixed GPA threshold or the prior year's class-rank cutoffs. If you can't find it, email your registrar directly — they'll have the information.

Step 2: Calculate Your Current GPA

Use Scholarics' GPA Calculator to get your current cumulative GPA based on all your coursework so far. Plug in your grades by semester or enter them individually — it takes about two minutes and gives you an accurate picture of where you stand.

Step 3: Project Your End-of-Degree GPA

This is where things get interesting. If you're a sophomore or junior, you have meaningful runway to move your GPA. Run different scenarios using Scholarics' GPA Simulator to see:

  • What GPA you'd need in your remaining semesters to hit a specific cumulative target
  • What happens to your GPA if you get one or two lower grades in harder courses
  • Whether it's realistic to move from, say, a 3.62 cumulative to a 3.70 magna threshold by senior year

The earlier you do this exercise, the more time you have to course-correct. Trying to raise a GPA significantly in your last two semesters is extremely difficult — the math works against you because early grades are already baked into the cumulative average.

Step 4: Understand What Your GPA Calculation Includes

Not all credits count equally for Latin honors purposes. At most schools, transfer credits are excluded from the GPA calculation used for honors. Pass/fail courses typically don't count. Some schools look only at upper-division credits (junior and senior level). The GPA used for honors calculations is typically the cumulative GPA across all courses for the entire degree, calculated at the time of graduation. Some schools use a final two-year GPA or a separate GPA calculation that excludes transfer credit. Confirm with your registrar which GPA is used for honors designation.

A Note on Minimum Credit Requirements

Nearly every school also has a residency requirement — you must have earned a certain number of credits at that institution to qualify for honors. At UConn, it's 54 credits. At UCLA, it's 90 units. At UMD, it's 60 hours. Transfer students especially should verify this early, since it affects whether they're even eligible.

How to List Latin Honors on Your Resume

Once you've earned an honor, make sure you're presenting it correctly. A few simple rules:

Where to Put It

Latin honors always go in the Education section of your resume — not in a separate accomplishments section, not under certifications. It belongs on the same line as your degree or directly beneath it.

Correct format:

Bachelor of Science in Economics, University of Michigan, 2025, Magna Cum Laude

or

Bachelor of Arts in Political Science, Georgetown University, 2025

Summa Cum Laude

After 2–3 years, most career advisors recommend keeping it brief as work experience becomes more relevant. It should always appear in the education section, not as a separate line item.

Capitalization and Italics

Latin honors are typically written in italics in formal documents (magna cum laude) or capitalized in resumes and LinkedIn profiles (Magna Cum Laude). Both formats are widely accepted. Don't write it in all-caps (MAGNA CUM LAUDE) — that looks like shouting and isn't standard.

On LinkedIn

Add it to the "Education" entry for your degree on LinkedIn. In the "Activities and societies" or "Description" field, you can write: "Graduated Magna Cum Laude." Most recruiters who search LinkedIn will see this when they view your profile.

GPA Alongside Honors?

You can include both — "Magna Cum Laude | GPA: 3.82" — but it's not required. Including your GPA alongside the honor adds context, especially if you're at a school where the honor might not be widely recognized. If your GPA is below the typical range people expect for that honor (because your school uses a percentile system), you might prefer to list just the honor.

What If Your School Doesn't Use Latin?

If your school uses "With Distinction," "High Honors," or another equivalent, list that directly. Add a brief parenthetical if you think it needs clarification: "Graduated With Highest Distinction (equivalent to Summa Cum Laude)."

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What GPA is cum laude at most schools?

The most common thresholds are: cum laude at 3.5, magna cum laude at 3.7, and summa cum laude at 3.9. These cutoffs are not universal, and each institution sets its own requirements. Some schools start cum laude as low as 3.2 (such as the University of Michigan's College of Engineering), while others don't award it until 3.9+ (at schools with high grade distributions like UCLA or UConn's Nursing program).

2. Is there a national standard for Latin honors GPA?

No. There is no national cutoff. Some universities set a fixed GPA; others award honors to a top percentage of each graduating class, so the number moves every year. The only way to know your target is to check with your specific school.

3. Can I lose Latin honors after I graduate?

No. Once your degree is conferred and the honor is printed on your diploma and transcript, it's permanent. You cannot lose a Latin honor designation after graduation as it is printed on your diploma and transcript. However, you can fail to qualify if your final GPA falls below the threshold before you graduate.

4. Do MIT and Stanford give Latin honors?

No. MIT does not award traditional Latin honors. Stanford also does not use traditional Latin honors. Both use alternative recognition systems. Stanford awards "With Distinction" to its top graduates; MIT has its own recognition system.

5. Does my major or school within the university affect my GPA requirement?

Frequently, yes. At large universities with multiple colleges (engineering, arts & sciences, business, nursing), each college often sets its own GPA cutoffs. Your threshold in the College of Engineering may be completely different from a student in the same graduating class studying English. Always check the specific cutoff for your college, not just the university-wide average.

6. Do Latin honors carry the same weight at every school?

Not really. Context matters enormously. A magna cum laude from Harvard means you finished in the top 20% of one of the world's most selective institutions. The same title from a school with lower admissions standards and a fixed 3.7 GPA threshold tells a different story. Sophisticated hiring managers and admissions officers understand this distinction, which is why it's sometimes worth briefly noting your GPA alongside the honor.

7. Can I earn Latin honors if I transferred from another school?

Maybe. Most schools require that you complete a minimum number of credits at their institution — typically 54 to 90 credit hours — to be eligible. Transfer credits usually don't count toward the GPA calculation for honors. Check with your registrar as early as possible if you transferred.

8. Does grad school or a second bachelor's degree qualify for Latin honors?

No. Latin honors are awarded to students who earned a bachelor's degree for academic merit; graduate students and postbaccalaureate students are generally not eligible. They apply to undergraduate degrees only.

9. Do pass/fail courses count toward my honors GPA?

Generally, no. Most schools exclude pass/fail (or S/U, satisfactory/unsatisfactory) grades from the GPA calculation used for Latin honors. If you took a course pass/fail, it typically won't help or hurt your honors eligibility, but it also won't increase your credit count toward any minimum requirements.

10. What happens if my GPA is right on the borderline?

Your GPA for Latin honors purposes is usually calculated precisely, without rounding. A 3.499 is not a 3.5, and a 3.699 is not a 3.7. Latin honors will be annotated in the commencement program as tentative and unofficial pending the submission and calculation of all final grades for the semester of commencement. Your final semester grades are what's used for the official determination.

11. How can I tell if I'm on track for honors?

Use Scholarics' GPA Calculator to find your current cumulative GPA, and the GPA Simulator to project forward. Input how many credit hours you have remaining, estimate your likely grades in upcoming courses, and the simulator will tell you what final cumulative GPA you're on track for. Many students are closer than they think — or further away — and knowing early makes a real difference.

12. Should I mention Latin honors in a job interview?

You don't need to bring it up unless you're asked directly about your academic background. If an interviewer asks about your GPA or academic performance, you can mention it naturally: "I finished magna cum laude, which at my school meant I was in the top ten percent of my class." Keep it brief and let the achievement speak for itself. Overemphasizing it — especially a few years into your career — can come across as trying too hard.
